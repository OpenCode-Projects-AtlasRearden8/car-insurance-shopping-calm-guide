# 05 — When Dropping Full Coverage Makes Sense

Chapter 01 defined "full coverage" (collision + comprehensive + liability).
This chapter is the subtraction chapter: when does it make sense to drop
collision, comprehensive, or both, happily and without regret?

## The 10% rule (with explanation)

The folk rule: **drop collision if the annual premium is more than 10% of the
car's actual cash value (ACV)**, after adjusting for the deductible.

Why ~10%? Because the expected payout on collision is essentially the *loss
probability times (ACV − deductible)*. If you'd more realistically never file a
claim, you're paying for nothing. The 10% heuristic is conservative; refine it:

```
Annual premium for collision          = $480
Car's realistic ACV (run KBB/NADA)    = $4,000
Deductible                            = $1,000
Worst-case recoverable claim          = $4,000 − $1,000 = $3,000
Premium ÷ realistic payout            = $480 / $3,000 = 16%
→ 16% is well above 10%: collision is expensive insurance here. Strong candidate to drop.
```

If the ratio is under ~10%, keep it. Between 10–16% is a judgment zone that
depends on cash savings and sleep-at-night factors.

## The real prerequisite: a savings buffer

Dropping collision means **self-insuring the deductible-to-ACV band yourself**
when you're at fault (or when the other driver is uninsured). You need the money
to either replace the car or repair it:

- If dropping saves $480/year, do you have ~$3,000 sitting somewhere that isn't
  emergency-critical? If yes: dropping is arithmetic. If no: keep it for now and
  revisit when the buffer exists.

## Four times dropping makes clear sense

1. **Hail-damage oldies.** Car worth $2,500, comprehensive $180/year with
   $500 deductible — often drop-ready, unless you live somewhere hail-heavy,
   where comprehensive is startlingly good value.
2. **The beater that works.** 2005 Camry worth $3,200; collision at $600/year.
   Trade: keep liability + UM/UIM, self-fund the $3,200 worst case. Strong.
3. **Annual-miles under ~3,000 with comprehensive-only risk profiles.** Low
   mileage means low crash risk; comprehensive (theft, weather) does much of
   the real pricing work. Drop collision, keep comprehensive — commonly a
   sensible middle called **"comp-only."**
4. **The car is your safest.** If the vehicle runs fine but any repair over
   ~$1,500 ends in "just total it," paying premium for a claim that ends in a
   check for less than the deductibles-and-premium combined may be pointless.

## Four times *not* to drop

1. **You still owe more than ACV on the loan.** The lender requires collision —
   and even if they didn't, gap coverage only exists with full coverage. Also
   don't drop gap until the loan dips below ACV (chapter 01).
2. **You literally could not function without the car.** Renters, four kids,
   rural no-buses, shift work. Collision plus rental reimbursement is partially
   insurance against life stopping.
3. **New-ish car (< ~5 years).** Until depreciation flattens out, collision is
   doing the heavy lifting.
4. **You'd lose bundle or multi-car discounts by re-structuring.** Sometimes
   dropping collision *changes* the rating tier and shifts discounts. Always
   re-quote both versions — the worksheet supports saving multiple versions.

## The middle options people forget

- **Comp-only** (drop collision, keep comprehensive): the most underrated move.
  Usually worth asking for.
- **Raise the deductible instead of dropping**: $500 → $1,000 commonly saves
  15–25% of collision premium, while still carrying the catastrophic part.
- **Lower the deductible and drop comprehensive instead** — older cars,
  low-theft-neighborhood version of the same idea.
- **State-specific "glass only" endorsements** keep free windshield fixes in
  several states even after comp stays or goes.

## The ACV misunderstanding that wrecks this decision

Insurers pay **actual cash value** — what a private buyer would pay for the car
today, not book magic and not what you owe. Everyone overestimates their car's
value; run real numbers (KBB private-party, NADA, or local listings on
Facebook Marketplace / AutoTrader) *before* the quote call, and write those
numbers on the worksheet.

## Quick worksheet for this decision

For one vehicle, note:

1. ACV = $______
2. Collision premium = $______ ; deductible = $______
3. Ratio: (2) ÷ [(1) − deductible] = ______%
4. Savings buffer available = $______ (yes / no)
5. Verdict — keep collision / comp-only / drop both / raise deductible

Below ~10% with buffer: probably keep.
Above ~10% with buffer: probably drop or comp-only.
This is a thinking aid, not a directive — your risk tolerance wins ties.
